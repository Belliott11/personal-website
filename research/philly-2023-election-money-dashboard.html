Philadelphia campaign finance · 2023 municipal elections

Who Bought the Mayor’s Race?

Two candidates spent a combined $30M+ of mostly their own money and both lost the primary. The candidate who became mayor raised less than a third of what the top self-funder spent — and got there on 3,520 separate contributions instead of one signature. The same pattern shows up further down the ballot, in City Council. Built from the city’s own campaign finance filings.

City Council At-Large: The Upset

Seven seats, elected citywide: five go to the top vote-getters overall, two are reserved for a minority party. For 70 years those two seats went to Republicans. In 2023 they went to the Working Families Party instead — and neither WFP winner outraised the Republican who lost the second seat by fewer than 5,000 votes.

District Council: Money Isn’t the Whole Race

Ten geographic seats, one winner each. Most 2023 incumbents faced no serious money race at all — but the one genuinely contested general election is the cleanest example on this whole page of fundraising not predicting the outcome.

Data & methods

  1. Source. Philadelphia Board of Ethics campaign finance filings, published as open data via OpenDataPhilly (tables campfin_contributions / campfin_expenditures). Scope here is every transaction reported in calendar year 2023 across the Mayor, City Council At-Large, and City Council district races — roughly 32,000 contribution records and 6,000 expenditure records combined.
  2. Donor classification (Mayor section). The source data doesn’t label most donors by type (87% of mayoral contribution rows are “Not Specified”), so donor category there is inferred: a contribution is self-funded when the donor name matches the candidate’s own name, a PAC / union / committee when the name matches keywords like PAC, union, committee, or fund, a business entity for LLC/Inc/Corp-style names, and individual otherwise. This is a heuristic, not a certified classification — a handful of edge cases (e.g. sole proprietorships named after their owner) will be misbucketed.
  3. Geography is the donor’s self-reported city/state on the filing, not employer location, and is missing for some smaller contributions.
  4. Totals raised vs. spent can diverge from a candidate’s full campaign because this dataset is scoped to 2023 filings only; a candidate who also raised money in 2022 (several did) can show 2023 spending in excess of 2023 raising.
  5. District council coverage is uneven. Districts 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 had incumbents who ran effectively unopposed in 2023 and show little or no itemized fundraising in this dataset — they’re summarized rather than charted. District 7’s incumbent (Quetcy Lozada) also has no itemized 2023 contributions on file here despite winning re-election.
  6. Election outcomes are not in the source dataset and were added from certified public results (Philadelphia City Commissioners, 2023 primary and general).
  7. A few donor and candidate names were corrected for character-encoding issues in the source export (e.g. Maria Quiñones-Sánchez, Erika Almirón).
